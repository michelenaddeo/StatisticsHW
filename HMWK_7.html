Modes of convergence in probability theory

A sequence of random variables can approach a limit in several different senses. This page defines the four main ones, shows with simulations how they differ, and explains which of them the Law of Large Numbers and the Central Limit Theorem use. It also explains why √n shows up.

What this page answers

  1. The differences between the modes of convergence — section 1 (definitions and implications) and section 3 (simulations, with counterexamples).
  2. Their roles in the LLN, the CLT and other results — section 2.
  3. Why √n appears, variance additivity and independence — section 4.

1The four modes of convergence

Xn is the n-th random variable of the sequence and X is the limit (often a constant).

ModeDefinitionIn words
In probability
Xn →P X		for every ε > 0: P(|Xn − X| > ε) → 0For large n, Xn is close to X with high probability. Large errors can still happen now and then, at later times too.
Almost surely
Xn →a.s. X		P( lim Xn = X ) = 1; equivalently P(supk≥n |Xk − X| > ε) → 0For almost every outcome, the whole sequence settles: from some point on it stays close to X forever.
Mean square (L²)
Xn →L² X		E[(Xn − X)²] → 0The average squared distance to X goes to 0. It also controls bias and variance of an estimator.
In distribution
Xn →d X		Fn(x) → F(x) at every continuity point x of FOnly the shape of the distribution settles; the values themselves need not get close to anything.

Implications

almost sure ⇒ in probability ⇒ in distribution
mean square (L²) ⇒ in probability
in distribution to a constant ⇒ in probability

Almost sure and mean-square convergence do not imply each other, and the arrows do not reverse in general. Section 3 gives an example for each missing arrow.

2Which mode in which theorem

ResultModeWhat it says
Weak law of large numbersin probabilityThe sample mean X̄n gets close to μ with probability tending to 1. With finite variance the proof is one line: Chebyshev and Var(X̄n) = σ²/n.
Mean-square versionL²E[(X̄n − μ)²] = σ²/n → 0. For an estimator, this is “mean squared error → 0”.
Strong law of large numbersalmost surelyFor almost every infinite run of experiments, the running average ends up at μ and stays there. It needs only E|X| < ∞.
Central limit theoremin distribution√n (X̄n − μ)/σ → N(0, 1). It describes the size and shape of the fluctuations around μ; it says nothing about a single sequence settling.
Other resultsmixedConsistency of an estimator (in probability or a.s.); asymptotic normality of the MLE (in distribution); Slutsky's lemma, continuous mapping and the delta method (they combine limits in probability and in distribution); Glivenko–Cantelli (the empirical CDF converges to F almost surely, uniformly).

In short: the LLN says where the average goes; the CLT says how it fluctuates around that point.

4Why √n, and the role of independence

Variance of a sum. Var(X1 + … + Xn) = Σ Var(Xi) + 2 Σi<j Cov(Xi, Xj). If the variables are independent (it is enough that they are uncorrelated) all the covariances vanish and the variances simply add: Var(Sn) = nσ². Hence sd(Sn) = σ√n.
Consequences. The sum Sn moves away from nμ by about σ√n, which is much less than n. Dividing by n squeezes the fluctuations to 0 (law of large numbers). Dividing by √n keeps them at a constant size σ, which is the scale at which a non-degenerate limit, the normal law, appears (CLT). The sample mean has standard error σ/√n.
In statistical inference. The precision of an average improves like 1/√n: to halve the standard error we need four times as much data. Confidence intervals X̄ ± z·σ/√n and test statistics √n(X̄ − μ0)/s are built on this.
Without independence. If every pair of variables has correlation ρ > 0, Var(Sn) = σ²(n + n(n−1)ρ), which grows like n²: the right scale is n, averaging no longer removes the error and the CLT with √n fails. In the extreme ρ = 1 all variables are equal and X̄n = X1. The “effective sample size” is n/(1 + (n−1)ρ).

Simulation: sd(Sn) for different correlations

Sums of n standard normal variables with common correlation ρ. For ρ = 0 the sd grows like √n (slope ½ on a log–log chart); for ρ > 0 it ends up growing like n (slope 1).

5Conclusions

  1. Strength of the modes. Almost sure and mean square are the strong ones, in probability is intermediate, in distribution is the weakest: it concerns distributions, not the variables themselves.
  2. LLN. The sample mean converges to μ in probability (weak law), in L² (finite variance) and almost surely (strong law).
  3. CLT. The standardised sum converges in distribution to the normal law; this is the mode that gives approximate probabilities, confidence intervals and tests.
  4. √n. It comes from the additivity of variance for independent summands; it is the scale of the fluctuations of a sum, and it disappears when observations are strongly dependent.

Conditional probability, Bayes' theorem and statistical inference

Bayes' theorem tells us how to change our beliefs when new data arrive. This page explains conditional probability, the three ingredients of Bayesian inference (prior, likelihood, posterior) and why updating is central, with two interactive examples.

What this page answers

  1. Conditional probability and Bayes' theorem — sections 1 and 2.
  2. Prior, likelihood, posterior, and why updating is central — section 2.
  3. Examples of priors and likelihoods — sections 3 and 4.
  4. Applications, advantages, limitations, influence on conclusions — section 5.

1Conditional probability

Definition. The probability of A given that B has occurred is P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B), for P(B) > 0. We restrict attention to the outcomes in B and ask which fraction of them also lies in A.

  • Multiplication rule: P(A ∩ B) = P(A | B)·P(B) = P(B | A)·P(A).
  • Total probability: if B1, …, Bk partition the sample space, P(A) = Σ P(A | Bi)·P(Bi).
  • Independence: A and B are independent exactly when P(A | B) = P(A): learning B changes nothing.
  • In statistics. A model gives the probability of the data given a parameter, p(data | θ). This is a conditional probability. Inference runs the other way: from the observed data to the parameter. Bayes' theorem is the bridge.

2Bayes' theorem: prior, likelihood, posterior

The theorem. From P(A ∩ B) = P(A | B)P(B) = P(B | A)P(A) we get
P(H | D) = P(D | H) · P(H) / P(D), with P(D) = Σ P(D | Hi) P(Hi)
H is a hypothesis (or a parameter value θ), D is the observed data.
Prior P(H). What we believe about H before seeing the data: earlier studies, expert knowledge, or a deliberately vague distribution.
Likelihood P(D | H). How probable the observed data are if H is true. As a function of H, with the data fixed, it is the likelihood function. It is not a probability distribution over H.
Posterior P(H | D). What we believe about H after seeing the data. It is the result of the update.
Evidence P(D). A normalising constant that makes the posterior sum (or integrate) to 1.
posterior ∝ likelihood × prior

Why updating is central. The posterior of today is the prior of tomorrow: each new batch of data refines the previous belief, in any order and in any batch size, and the final result is the same. Bayesian inference also gives direct probability statements about the quantity of interest (“the probability that θ lies between 0.4 and 0.8 is 95%”), which the frequentist approach does not.

5Priors and likelihoods in practice

ChoiceAdvantagesLimitations and influence on conclusions
Informative prior
(earlier studies, expert knowledge)		Uses existing information; stabilises estimates with little data; very useful in medicine, quality control and small samples.It can dominate when n is small: with a Beta(20, 20) prior and 10 flips, the posterior stays close to ½. A wrong prior is slow to be corrected (try the optimistic prior).
Flat or vague priorLets the data speak; the posterior mean is close to the sample proportion.“No information” is not unique: flat on θ is not flat on θ² or on the odds. Improper priors can break the posterior.
Likelihood (the model)Carries all the information of the data about θ (shared with frequentist methods: the MLE maximises it).If the model is wrong, the conclusions are wrong whatever the prior. Complex models need simulation (MCMC).
Many dataPosterior ≈ likelihood: different priors lead to nearly the same conclusion (see the four-prior table).With few data the answers differ, so the choice of prior should be stated and tested (sensitivity analysis).
  • Credible versus confidence interval. A 95% credible interval is a statement about θ given the data (“θ is in the interval with probability 0.95”). A frequentist 95% confidence interval is a property of the procedure over repeated samples.
  • Applications. Diagnostic tests and screening, spam filters, A/B testing, estimation with small samples, combining several studies, sequential decisions.

6Conclusions

  1. Conditional probability is the language of both statistics (p(data | θ)) and of learning (P(H | data)).
  2. Bayes' theorem turns prior and likelihood into the posterior: posterior ∝ likelihood × prior. The posterior can be updated again with new data.
  3. Prior information matters most when data are few; as data accumulate the posterior is dominated by the likelihood. Always report and test the prior.
  4. A positive test for a rare disease is far from conclusive (section 3): ignoring the prior leads to wrong conclusions.
